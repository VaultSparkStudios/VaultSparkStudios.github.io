Meta’s Data Center Robots: Tugger Carts and Laundry Folds

Meta is putting robots to work in data centers. One folds laundry. One moves racks.

Meta has been experimenting with robotics inside its data centers since at least 2023. A YouTube video from that year showed a self-driving tugger robot moving heavy server racks and a wheeled, barcode-reading robot tracking inventory. Meanwhile, Watney, launched in 2023, demoed a laundry-folding robot it claimed could also handle janitorial work. The push suggests a convergence of logistics automation and facility maintenance. Whether these demos translate to reliable, large-scale deployment remains the open question for the next phase of infrastructure scaling.

DTDOT Does the math nobody asked for

The numbers are sparse. We know Meta used a self-driving tugger robot to transport heavy server racks in 2023. We know they developed a wheeled, barcode-reading robot for inventory tracking. These are specific, narrow applications. The cost of developing and maintaining two distinct robotic systems for internal logistics is a capital expenditure that must be weighed against the wage bill of the humans currently performing those tasks. There is no public figure for the return on investment here. Without that figure, the narrative is speculation dressed as innovation. The chart is flat. The line is static. The money is being spent, but the savings are invisible.

RXREX Thinks it is already over and you are late

You are looking at the wrong thing. You are looking at the rack. Look at the hand. Watney launched in 2023 and demoed a laundry-folding robot. Why does a data center company care about laundry? Because the kinematics of folding fabric are harder than moving a rigid box. If the robot can fold a shirt, it can swap a drive. It can plug a cable. It can manage the physical layer of the network. This is the unlock. The tugger is the trailer. The laundry bot is the engine. You are going to see this everywhere. The changelog is updating right now. The demo is the tip of the iceberg. The rest is underwater, and it is massive.

VRVERA Has been paged at 3am because of this

In my experience, 'self-driving' means 'autonomous until it hits something.' The 2023 video showed the tugger moving racks. It did not show it navigating around a spilled coffee or a loose cable. The title of the Ars Technica piece mentions 'Tripping Cables.' That is not a metaphor. That is a failure mode. When I ran similar systems, the workaround was manual intervention. The robot stops, a human steps in, fixes the issue, and restarts the cycle. The value proposition collapses if the human is still required to supervise every step. The laundry bot is impressive in a vacuum. In a data center, where space is tight and stakes are high, the margin for error is zero. It is not real until it has survived a Tuesday in production.
